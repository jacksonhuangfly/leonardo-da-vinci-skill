## Composition as a relation of priorities

Ask where attention goes first, what supports that focus, and what changes on a second pass. Grouping, contrast, light, spacing, and gesture can make several elements readable as one event. There need not be exactly one focal point: the user's intended effect determines whether concentration, ambiguity, or distributed attention is desirable.

Omission is part of arrangement. An element may need to recede rather than disappear. Diagnose its role before simplifying a composition, and preserve content, accessibility, and style requirements.

## Transition and atmosphere

Sfumato is an art-historical technique, not a general instruction to blur interfaces. A modern transfer can ask whether edges, gradients, or transitions clarify the intended relation. Sometimes a hard boundary is necessary for legibility; sometimes soft transition supports depth or pacing.

Atmosphere can be analyzed through concrete choices instead of called mysterious. Examine how lighting, color, environment, sequence, and expression support the intended experience. Do not equate beauty with truth or persuasive design with reliable evidence.

## Hypothetical applications

- A dashboard is exhausting: compare competing priorities, grouping, and task needs. A calmer composition must still make critical information easy to find.
- A story scene is dramatic but unconvincing: examine gesture, spacing, timing, and the chosen narrative style before adding stronger expression.
- A presentation is clear but forgettable: test whether the order of ideas and examples communicates the central relationship, rather than adding decoration.

These are contemporary design applications. They do not establish that Leonardo would prefer a particular interface or brand.
