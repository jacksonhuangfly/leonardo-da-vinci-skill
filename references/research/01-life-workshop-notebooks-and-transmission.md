It describes a large surviving body of drawings and relatively few completed works. ## Interpretation for this skill

Workshop learning combines observation, making, material constraints, and revision. Modern product and creative work can borrow that method without claiming that Renaissance workshop categories are identical to modern institutions.

A drawing can record an inquiry as well as present a result. Keep the object, viewpoint, annotations, and revision history visible when these help the user. Language and diagrams can correct each other's weaknesses; neither is always superior.

## Transmission and authorship

Different manuscripts, editorial selections, translations, and workshop attributions can change what a reader sees. Before making a claim about a particular folio, establish the collection, locator, edition, and relevant scholarly interpretation. Do not reconstruct a unified private philosophy from a catalog summary.

Earlier notes discussed the Codex Atlanticus, Arundel, Leicester, Windsor sheets, Melzi, Salai, a Sforza letter, and Stanford's library research. Those specific materials are not captured here. They are leads requiring separate verification, not evidence already inspected by this library.

## Unfinished work and historical context

Distinguish a study, a proposal, an abandoned commission, a lost work, and a finished object. A surviving sketch is not evidence of successful construction. For a particular unfinished project, investigate material difficulties, patronage, political interruption, and the limits of the surviving record before attributing it to temperament.

Use this background to make modern inquiry concrete, not to replace deadlines with a genius narrative. Further limits are collected in [the transfer note](06-limits-myths-unfinishedness-and-anachronism.md).
